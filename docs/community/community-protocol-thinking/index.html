#From Code Abundance to Human Agency

The ideas and principles behind the TypeRoof Community Protocol

If you want to discuss this text, join the discussion thread.

#A technological shift

Whether we like it or not, code is becoming abundant.

For me and many programmers in my bubble, “AI” arrived in our workflows with a bang in early 2026. Just a few months earlier, in the fourth quarter of 2025, we were still joking about the vibe-coded mess the word-probability wiggling was producing. Then around Christmas and New Year, or by the first quarter of 2026 at the latest, it suddenly became obviously useful in programming.

I’m not necessarily a proponent or opponent of the technology. I would also have preferred it if we had never entered the atomic age or begun burning fossil fuels. (I like the wheel, though!) But here we are. Technologies of this scale seep into every corner of life, get weaponized, and their consequences affect everyone. My personal assessment is: they are here to stay. Pandora’s box has been opened once again. So I’m choosing not to fight it, despite there being good reasons to do so. Instead, this is an honest attempt to make the best of it, for my fellow humans and colleagues, for typography, and for the Libre Software Movement.

#A human challenge

Capability doesn’t automatically create Autonomy.

Now, anyone can prompt code into existence. People write less and less code directly. People who can’t read or write code at all now create tools and features just on the fly. For them, this technology is an empowerment, and for me it is too: I like the idea of finally getting to all the code I always wanted to write. I can read and write code, and I enjoy it, but I can no longer afford the luxury of taking my time and writing it all myself. There is no single reason; my personal life is more complex than it used to be, and the world is simply spinning faster now.

When it comes to code an agent has written, even in a tight interactive loop, I feel code alienation. I’m sure I’m not alone in this. Before, I was confident that I had thought through and integrated every line of code in my own projects, with all the relevant information in my mind as I wrote it. I could look at my code and trust my past self to have made the best possible decision at that time. This is gone now. In some ways, I’m glad I never worked much on projects with large engineering teams. I was fortunate in that respect, and I can see how this alienation existed in big tech long before I experienced it myself. But here we are: when I generate code by prompting, I lose touch; it feels less like my code and more like any code.

Ownership is at the heart of this—not so much in terms of legal details, but rather in terms of responsibility and value. This is my code; I want it to be high-value, well-engineered, and trustworthy. I stand behind this code with my name, because I own it. Or, perhaps more precisely, because I understand it well enough to take responsibility for it. Collective ownership works similarly, but it requires more trust in the processes through which the software is made and in the collective itself, since there is no longer a single source of trust.

#A human objective

While I’m no psychologist, I’ll use Self-Determination Theory (SDT) as a guiding principle; it describes Autonomy, Competence, and Relatedness as basic psychological needs that, when satisfied, help foster intrinsic motivation and well-being. I think these ideas can help us navigate what our community could become for its members. Alongside this, I find self-efficacy—a person’s belief in their own abilities—to be a great concept to keep in mind. The three phases of the Community Protocol are where these needs are meant to be met; the three pillars—Contributor Experience, Trust, and FLOSS—are the foundation that makes this possible.

The cost of producing code is collapsing, while the cost of understanding it is not. Writing code by hand is losing economic value, and the craft is shifting from writing code to understanding it. All this is happening while expectations for project completeness and the pace of shipping features are rising. This creates a direct path to technical debt and overwhelming complexity when the capacity to understand and maintain the software doesn’t keep pace with production speed. And when understanding falls behind, so does the psychological meaning of the work and that vital feeling of accomplishment—this is where code alienation sets in.

When anyone can clone a project or prompt a feature in isolation, we have to rethink why we collaborate.

#An organizational response

When code is infinite and instantly generated, human attention, direction, and trust become the rarest, most valuable resources.

Here is the bet: Incubation invests two of these, attention and direction, to produce the third: trust. We don’t try to keep up with the possible flood of code from Inception; instead, we choose, deliberately and sparingly, what deserves our attention, and we give it fully. Incubation is pull-based for exactly this reason: each distribution selects only as much as it can genuinely understand and stand behind, and nobody can push unrequested work onto Maintainers.

This three-phase structure is designed to give us back our agency, to develop with intent, and to ensure we maintain only what we trust is worth our ownership. While old sources of individual purpose erode, we create an environment that nurtures basic human needs and gives grounding.

#Inception

Driven by Autonomy and Relatedness, this phase allows anyone to experiment, play, share, and discuss ideas without the heavy burden of maintenance. We call these contributors Catalysts. Domain experts—type designers, typographers, script and language experts—can spark projects through their sheer Competence. You choose exactly how you want to participate.

#Incubation

This phase focuses on developing and expressing Competence and self-efficacy. Through mentorship and collaboration, Builders take the time to refine raw concepts into trustworthy, production-ready features. The Relatedness of working alongside a Maintainer—guided by the standards of a target distribution—aims to ensure that both parties truly understand the code they shape, restoring the pride and ownership that automated generation tends to strip away. Ultimately, this shared effort is what builds the trust we bet on.

#Distribution

Here, the focus shifts to collective Autonomy and shared stewardship. Because different distributions can operate entirely on their own terms, Maintainers have the freedom to curate standards that fit their specific users. It is their choice how they handle the modern influx of code: a distribution might decide to act as a deliberate shield against tech debt, prioritizing deep understanding and stability, while another might optimize for rapid, cutting-edge integration. This model anchors the ecosystem in deep Relatedness—relying on human trust, architectural care, and mutual growth to sustain software that we can all stand behind.

#The critical mechanism

In a world where new features can be generated or patched together in an instant, raw capability is no longer the bottleneck. What is rare is understanding. A Proposal might begin as a prompted script, a handcrafted human prototype, the integration of an existing app, or simply a raw idea. Regardless of its origin, the Incubation phase is our critical mechanism for converting this capability into genuine responsibility. When a Builder and a Maintainer work together to refine these rough concepts, they are doing more than just merging code: they are bridging the gap between mere creation and deliberate architectural intent, and building real Competence.

This phase will be the most challenging to pull off. It is a novel approach, and we will have to find our way around as we put it into practice. Yet, this shared friction is meant to make every feature that passes through it deeply understood, explicitly owned, and fully trusted.

#The lasting foundation

TypeRoof’s core is licensed under Apache 2.0 on purpose: its permissive nature removes friction from the ecosystem, so features and improvements can move freely between the core and its distributions. Distributions remain free to choose their own compatible license—ideally, improvements can and will flow back to the core.

TypeRoof’s users shall be safe from vendor lock-in and corporate capture. The structure is designed to ensure this: because anyone can launch an autonomous distribution, users always have a choice. If one distribution stops serving their needs, they can move to another that shares the same underlying ecosystem. The four freedoms, and a DCO that keeps contributors’ copyright with them, ensure that no steward, including the company behind the core, can ever take that away. What protects and sustains TypeRoof is a strong community: trust, curation, and human relationships.

#The larger aspiration

TypeRoof is ultimately about building foundational type tooling infrastructure. Many of its concepts are already turning the wheel of digital typography; the larger revolution is to apply them to any script in any language, and to end typographic disadvantage. That goal is far bigger than one company or one group of programmers can reach alone. It needs the people who know those scripts and languages, and it needs an ecosystem in which their contributions can be trusted, remain freely accessible, and last.

So this is the hope: that even in the face of a massive, disruptive technological shift, we can choose how we organize ourselves; that we do not have to accept code alienation or a race to the bottom; and that technology stays an instrument of human agency rather than a replacement for it. We are building software, and we are building a space for mutual growth—because the first will not get far without the second.
